# METHOD — the sentence-island system (and what's actually true about it)

Source: Mikel (Hyperpolyglot), *"I speak 12 languages - copy my 30 min learning routine"*,
2026-04-10. He sells an app (hyperlingua.app) built on this method; the video is
also an ad for it. That does not make the method wrong — most of it is real
technique with research behind it — but it explains the overcorrections. This file
separates the two: the method as he states it, then what holds up, then how it
attaches to the book ladders already in this folder.

---

## The method

### Step 1 — Build your sentence list ("language islands")

Not words. **Full sentences you would actually say**, drawn from your own life.

Practically: for a few days, narrate your day out loud and capture it with
speech-to-text. What you say at work, what you order, what you complain about,
what stories you retell, what your opinions are. After a few days you have pages
of text that is *yours*. Translate it into the target language (AI is fine for
this), and that becomes the curriculum — a few thousand sentences that are all
sentences you have a real reason to say.

The claimed side effect: you absorb grammar from hundreds of worked examples
instead of studying rules, and after a few hundred sentences you start feeling
what sounds right.

Capture prompts are in [Sentence Islands/capture-prompts.md](Sentence%20Islands/capture-prompts.md).

### Step 2 — Flood your ears, then shadow

Turn every sentence into audio (TTS is fine if the voice is natural). Play it in
all your dead time: commute, dishes, gym, getting dressed. Goal: you can predict
the next sentence before it plays.

Once you understand a sentence easily, stop listening passively and **shadow** it
— say it out loud simultaneously with the audio. This is what moves listening
into speaking: mouth muscle memory, rhythm, full native speed with no slowdown.

Self-check on pronunciation: record yourself, compare against the source audio,
adjust. Beyond that, do not obsess — communication beats accent.

### Step 3 — Active recall (the non-negotiable)

Look at the English sentence, do **not** look at the translation, say the target
sentence out loud from memory, then check. Getting it wrong and then seeing the
answer is the part that builds the memory. Listening and shadowing are still
semi-passive; this is production from scratch.

15 focused minutes a day is enough if you are genuinely pushing; multiple small
sessions beat one long one; a session before sleep gets a consolidation bonus.

**Advanced version:** role-play your own life in the language all day. Narrate
the email you are about to send, rehearse the coffee order, complain in the target
language first.

### Bonus — pre-input comprehension

Before watching or listening to native content, get the transcript, study it,
learn the new words — *then* watch. You go from ~10% comprehension (which teaches
nothing) to 70-90% (where every sentence reinforces something you just learned).
This is what makes real native content usable months earlier than it otherwise is.

### The daily routine as he lays it out

| When | What | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Getting ready | Sentence audio playing, shadowing | ~20 min |
| Commute | Listening / shadowing (or silent if in public) | 30-40 min |
| Lunch break | Active recall, high intensity | 10-15 min |
| Commute home | Listening; talk to yourself using the sentences | 20-30 min |
| Before bed | Serious active recall session | 20 min to 2 hr |

Net new time carved out of the day: ~30 minutes. Everything else rides on time
that was already spent doing something else.

His progress claim: week 1 brutal, week 2 about 20-30% recall without checking,
week 3 patterns click and latency drops, week 4 functional basic conversation on
prepared topics, week 6 genuinely conversational.

## What holds up, and what doesn't

**Strongest claim, best supported: active recall.** The testing effect is one of
the most replicated findings in learning research (Roediger & Karpicke 2006 and
the large literature after it) — retrieval practice beats re-exposure by a wide
margin, and the difficulty is the mechanism, not a side effect. He is right that
this is the non-negotiable, and right that it is the step people skip.

**Shadowing is real technique, not a YouTube invention.** It is standard in
conference-interpreter training. The pinned comment from a working Japanese
interpreter is consistent with how that field actually trains.

**Personalized sentences over word lists: correct in practice.** Frequency lists
optimize for the average speaker; you are not the average speaker. Learning
chunks rather than isolated words also matches how formulaic language actually
gets stored and produced.

**Pre-input comprehension: the best idea in the video.** Studying a transcript
before consuming the content is the pedagogically standard intensive/extensive
pairing, and the 10%-comprehension problem he describes is genuine — input far
above your level is close to noise.

**"You don't need grammar at all" — overcorrection.** The child-acquisition
analogy is bad: children get tens of thousands of hours, a different neural
window, and constant corrective interaction. For adults, meta-analyses of
explicit instruction (Norris & Ortega 2000; Spada & Tomita 2010) find a
measurable positive effect on accuracy, with explicit teaching outperforming
purely implicit exposure. What is true: grammar study alone does not produce
speech, and it is where procrastinators hide. Correct use is as a lookup tool and
an error-correction tool — you hit a pattern you cannot generalize, you read two
pages about it, you go back to sentences. Throwing the books away leaves you able
to say your memorized sentences and unable to build a sentence you have never
seen.

**"Apps are a waste of time" — partly fair, and self-serving.** The critique of
streak-gamified apps as a *sole* method is legitimate. The video then closes by
selling his app. Treat the whole apps segment as competitive positioning.

**"Just immerse yourself doesn't work" — he is arguing against himself.** Step 2
*is* immersion; he has just narrowed it to material he authored. Input is
necessary and not sufficient — that is the honest version. Unstructured input at
low comprehension is inefficient, which is exactly the problem his own bonus
technique fixes.

**"Six weeks to conversational" — true only for easy languages, and he never
mentions difficulty.** This is the biggest omission for this folder. FSI's own
data puts Spanish, French and Portuguese at roughly 600-750 class hours to
professional working proficiency; Russian, Hebrew and Tagalog sit in the middle
tier; Arabic, Chinese, Japanese (and Farsi, somewhat less) sit at ~2200 hours.
Six of the ten languages here are in the harder tiers. The method still works in
all of them — the timeline does not transfer. Expect the six-week outcome in
Spanish and Portuguese, and expect the same method to take three to five times
longer in Japanese, Chinese or Arabic, before script learning is even counted.

**The method's real blind spot: comprehension of speech you did not write.**
Islands make you a strong producer on your own topics fast, and the first time a
native speaker answers you with a sentence you never scripted, you are lost. His
step 2 only feeds you your own sentences back. This is why the input engines in
the per-language READMEs stay in the plan rather than getting deleted.

**Also unstated: AI translation of your sentences will contain errors** —
register slips, over-literal renderings, textbook forms nobody says. For Spanish
or French this is minor. For Japanese (politeness levels), Arabic (dialect vs.
MSA — a machine will happily hand you MSA sentences no one speaks
conversationally) or Farsi (formal vs. colloquial), an unchecked AI sentence list
will teach you to sound wrong in a way that is hard to unlearn. Get the first
hundred sentences checked by a tutor or a native speaker before you drill them
into permanent memory.

## How this attaches to the plan already in this folder

The [README](README.md) ladders are book-first: one fundamental book per language,
plus an input engine, plus speaking. The video says throw the book away. Neither
extreme is right, and they are solving different problems:

- **The book gives you generative capacity** — the ability to construct a sentence
  you have never heard. That is what islands alone cannot do.
- **Islands give you fluency on your own life** — instant, automatic, no-latency
  production of the things you specifically need to say, which is what books
  famously fail to deliver.
- **The input engine gives you comprehension of other people**, which neither of
  the other two delivers.

The revised split, per day, for whichever language is in focus:

| Component | Share | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Active recall on your sentence list | ~50% of focused time | Step 3 above. The engine. |
| Book / grammar spine | ~30% of focused time | The fundamental book from the language README, worked normally — but when it conflicts with drilling, drilling wins. |
| Input engine | dead time + ~20% | The graded listening/reading from the language README, plus pre-input comprehension on anything native. |
| Sentence audio | all remaining dead time | Costs no scheduled time. |

Maintenance languages (everything not in focus) do not get islands work — they
get the shared daily Anki session already described in the README. Islands are a
focus-language tool; running ten sentence lists at once is exactly the trap the
README's rotation exists to prevent.
