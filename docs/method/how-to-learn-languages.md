# How To Learn Languages — the method, the dose, and the honest cost

Distilled 2026-08-08 from `../_Research/2026-08-08 - Language Learning Research.md` (nine
meta-analyses, effect sizes and honesty labels in the source). Read `../How To Learn.md` first for
the general learning hierarchy; this file is the language-specific layer, and it **corrects three
things** that file and the folder READMEs currently say. Corrections are marked ⚠.

---

## 1. The four engines — and nothing else

Instructed SLA is unusually well-measured. Four interventions have meta-analytic support at
medium-to-large effect sizes. Almost everything else the internet argues about does not.

| Engine | Effect | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Explicit instruction** — a grammar book, worked exercises, direct explanation | **g ≈ 1.06** | Kang, Sok & Han 2019 (54 studies, 5,051 learners) |
| **Interaction with prompted correction** — a tutor who stops you and makes you say it right | **d = 1.09** interaction; **d = 0.83** prompts vs **0.53** recasts | Mackey & Goo 2007; Lyster & Saito 2010 |
| **Spaced retrieval of high-frequency vocabulary** | medium-to-large; longer intervals win at delay | Kim & Webb 2022 (98 ES, 48 experiments) |
| **Comprehensible input, captioned** | d = 0.46 extensive reading; large for captions | Nakanishi 2015; Montero Perez et al. 2013 |

Plus one cheap, front-loadable bonus almost nobody uses — **§4**.

The most important structural point: **explicit study is not the compromise you make because you
can't move to the country.** At g ≈ 1.06 it is the largest single effect in the field, and
*intensity of instruction* was a significant moderator in both major meta-analyses. Concentrated
beats sprinkled. The one-book-per-folder design already in `Books & Fundamentals.md` is the
evidence-backed structure; the failure mode is doing it thinly across ten fronts.

⚠ **Correction 1 — do not delay speaking.** `Japanese/Japanese Learning Research.md` records
Refold's input-first, output-at-Phase-4 roadmap, and Dreaming Spanish recommends silence for
~600 hours. That is contradicted by the interaction and feedback meta-analyses above, and by
Swain's Canadian French immersion finding: students with *thousands of hours* of comprehensible
input reached near-native comprehension while their spoken grammar stayed persistently inaccurate.
The defensible version is **comprehension leads production** — not that production waits for an
hour count. Start speaking at roughly week 6, badly.

## 2. The number that turns "fluency" into a plan

Nation (2006), corpus-analytic, the most quantified target in this whole library:

| Vocabulary (word families) | What it buys |
|---|---|
| **1,000** | ~75–80% of written text, ~80% of speech. Not enough to understand anything unassisted. |
| **2,000** | ~80% written, **~95–96% of informal spoken** language. Conversation becomes possible. |
| **4,000–5,000** | **95% coverage** — the minimum comprehension threshold. |
| **6,000–7,000** | 98% of *spoken* text. |
| **8,000–9,000** | 98% of *written* text — unassisted reading. |

Two consequences. First, the curve is violently front-loaded: the first 1,000 families do 75–80% of
the work, and bands 2,001–3,000 add only 2–4%. Second, **the interesting range is 2,000 → 5,000** —
that is where a language stops being a hobby and becomes usable, and it is the range every dabbler
quits before reaching.

Plan every language against this table, not against an hour count.

## 3. Vocabulary: how to actually run the SRS

- **Deliberate flashcard learning produces real lexical knowledge.** Elgort 2011 showed
  flashcard-learned items generating form priming, masked repetition priming, and automatic
  semantic priming — processed *more* automatically than low-frequency real L2 words. The community
  claim that "Anki words aren't real words" is false.
- **But unreviewed gains collapse.** Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara 2020: immediate recall 60.1%
  meaning / 58.5% form → delayed **39.4% / 25.1%**. Productive knowledge decays hardest. Spacing is
  not an optimization, it is the thing that stops three-quarters of the work evaporating.
- **Flashcards and word lists are the *most efficient* intentional activity** (77.0% and 73.2%
  immediate meaning recall in the same meta-analysis). Not a shameful shortcut — the best-measured
  option.
- **Retrieval count floor:** 5–7 retrievals beat 1–3 on both immediate and delayed tests (Nakata 2017).
- **Equal vs expanding intervals is a non-issue.** Kim & Webb 2022 found them statistically
  equivalent. What matters: spacing at all, and *longer* intervals for durable retention. Stop
  tuning the algorithm. (FSRS over SM-2 is still the right default — ~20–30% fewer reviews for
  equal retention in the open benchmark — but that is simulation on logged data, not a trial.)
- **Looking a word up is not the learning event.** Yanagisawa & Webb 2021: of the involvement-load
  components, *evaluation* contributed most, *need* second, and **search contributed nothing**.
  A lookup teaches you little; deciding whether the word fits *this* sentence teaches you a lot.
  Cards should force a judgement, not a recognition.
- **Incidental acquisition from input is slow:** r = .34 between encounters and learning, with
  estimates from 6 to 20+ encounters and no magic number (Uchihara et al. 2019). Input is how you
  consolidate and how you get the long tail. It is a bad way to acquire the frequent core — do that
  deliberately.

⚠ **Correction 2 — card direction should change with level.** `../How To Learn.md` currently
prescribes production cards (English prompt → produce the L2) from the start. SSLA 2021 found
**L2→L1 more effective for lower-proficiency learners** and **L1→L2 more effective at higher
proficiency**. So: recognition direction while you are a beginner, flip to production around the
point the grammar spine is finished. That is the moderator that explains why prior studies
disagreed.

**Deck policy:** one frequency-ordered core deck per language (top 1,000 → 2,000 → 5,000), sentence
cards with audio once past the first thousand, 20 new/day in push mode, **0 new/day in maintenance
mode**. Do not interleave vocabulary across languages within a session — interleaving is verified
null-to-negative for vocabulary (`../How To Learn.md`).

## 4. Pronunciation — the strong finding nobody uses

- Explicit pronunciation instruction: **d = 0.89** within-group, **d = 0.80** between-group
  (Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015, 86 reports). Larger with **longer** interventions and with **feedback**.
- **High Variability Phonetic Training** — drilling perception of a contrast spoken by *many
  different voices*: **g = 0.92** pre-post and **g = 0.67** vs control on perception, with retention
  and generalization to novel stimuli; **g = 0.77** on production in a second meta-analysis.
  Effects largest for **consonants and lexical tones**, and largest for **beginners (g = 0.90)**.

Training your ear changes your mouth. This is cheap, it front-loads, and perception errors
otherwise fossilize into every single word you subsequently learn. For **Chinese** (tones),
**Arabic** and **Hebrew** (emphatics, pharyngeals), and **Russian** (palatalization) this is the
highest-leverage two weeks available. Concretely: minimal-pair identification drills with multiple
speakers, 10 min/day for the first fortnight, then twice weekly.

## 5. Input — with one correction

- **Extensive reading works**, d = 0.46 group / 0.71 pre-post, with longer programs doing better
  (Nakanishi 2015).
- **Captions produce large gains** in both listening comprehension and vocabulary, at every
  proficiency level (Montero Perez et al. 2013). Target-language subtitles on target-language
  video, always.
- **Listening strategy instruction**: d = 0.69 (Dalman & Plonsky 2025).

⚠ **Correction 3 — reading-while-listening is not a comprehension multiplier.** A 2023
meta-analysis (30 studies, N = 1,945) found g = **0.18** overall, and **g = 0.06 when self-paced** —
which a solo learner always is. For higher-proficiency learners, reading-only sometimes beat it
(split attention). Keep the practice for what it does do: forcing pace, and training
sound-to-spelling mapping. Do not treat it as an upgrade over reading.

## 6. Sleep

New since the 2026-07-17 run. `../How To Learn.md` records that sleep-consolidation claims did not
survive verification — true for the *general* learning literature. For **vocabulary specifically**
there is a dedicated evidence base: word meanings are retained better across 12 hours including
sleep than 12 waking hours, and at least one study found interleaving sleep between sessions halved
the practice required. L2 words consolidate more slowly than L1 words. **MODERATE** (lab novel-word
paradigms).

Practical: **run the SRS session in the evening**, not the morning, when you are pushing a language.
It costs nothing to move.

## 7. What the FSI hours actually are

Second research run in a row that refused to verify them, and the verdict is unchanged. The FSI
categories describe **full-time, ≤6-student, aptitude-screened training of adult diplomats toward a
professional rating**. Converting weeks → hours → a self-study timeline compounds several
unvalidated assumptions, and every accessible source on the web is SEO content citing other SEO
content.

**Use FSI as an ordinal difficulty ranking and nothing else.** Same for the Dreaming Spanish
300/600/1,000/1,500-hour milestones — the roadmap itself calls them general estimations. They are a
useful planning heuristic; they are not evidence, and DS's silence recommendation is contradicted
outright (§1).

Also worth knowing, since it cuts the other way: **aptitude has low predictive validity for
vocabulary learning** and matters most at the initial stages of explicit learning (Li 2015). The
part of the job that consumes the most hours is the part aptitude predicts least. And the sharp
critical-period cliff at 17.4 was an artifact of pooling learner types (van der Slik et al. 2022) —
the decline is real, gradual, about *grammar*, and says nothing about vocabulary or (per §4)
trainable pronunciation.

## Reconciling this with METHOD.md and the sentence islands

`METHOD.md` arrived the same day as this file, from a different source (a hyperpolyglot's
routine). Most of it survives contact with the evidence above; three points need adjusting, and
one apparent conflict is not a conflict.

**The frequency-list-vs-personal-sentences argument dissolves once you name the goal.**
METHOD.md argues frequency lists optimize for the average speaker and you are not average. True —
*for production*. But §2's coverage table is about **comprehension**, and comprehension of speech
you did not write is precisely the blind spot METHOD.md identifies in its own system. The two tools
solve different problems and both are needed:

| Tool | Buys you | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Personal sentence islands | Instant, no-latency production of *your* life | Involvement Load: *evaluation* is the active ingredient, and self-authored sentences are pure evaluation |
| Frequency-ordered core deck | Understanding *other people* | Nation 2006 coverage curve — 1,000 families ≈ 75–80%, 5,000 ≈ 95% |

Notably, Yanagisawa & Webb 2021 actively **supports** METHOD.md's step 1: of the three
involvement-load components, *search* contributed nothing to learning and *evaluation* contributed
most. Writing a sentence you actually mean is maximal evaluation. Islands are well-founded — they
are just not a substitute for coverage.

**Pre-input comprehension is the best idea in METHOD.md, and it now has numbers behind it.**
Studying a transcript before watching is exactly the move from *below* the 95% minimum
comprehension threshold to *above* the 98% unassisted-reading threshold (Nation 2006). That is the
quantitative version of "10% comprehension teaches nothing." Keep doing it; it is the cheapest way
to make native content usable months early.

**Active recall is right, and now it has a dose.** 5–7 retrievals beat 1–3 on both immediate and
delayed tests (Nakata 2017), and a session before sleep is genuinely better for vocabulary (§6) —
METHOD.md guessed right on both. One scope note: the L2→L1-for-beginners finding in §3 comes from
paired-associate **vocabulary** research, so it governs the frequency deck. Island recall is
explicitly production training, where L1→L2 is the point; leave step 3 as written.

**⚠ "Do not obsess about pronunciation" is the one place METHOD.md is clearly wrong.**
Explicit pronunciation instruction runs d = 0.89, HVPT runs g = 0.92 on perception and g = 0.77 on
production, and effects are **largest for beginners** — while perception errors fossilize into
every word learned afterward. Worse, shadowing a single TTS voice is the *low*-variability version
of the exact training that works: **number of talkers is a meta-analytic moderator**. The fix is
nearly free — generate the sentence audio in several different voices rather than one, and spend
the first fortnight on minimal pairs (§4).

**⚠ Shadowing's evidence is thinner than METHOD.md implies.** There is no meta-analysis. The best
study (Hamada 2016) found phoneme-perception gains in both proficiency groups but
listening-comprehension gains **only for low-proficiency learners**. Its provenance in
conference-interpreter training is real but is not efficacy evidence for L2 learners. Keep it as a
beginner bottom-up decoding drill; do not expect it to carry comprehension.

**⚠ The AI-translation warning is right and the fix is specifiable.** Get the first hundred
sentences checked by a tutor with the §10 instruction — prompted correction is d = 0.83 versus
d = 0.53 for a tutor who just reads the good version back at you. And METHOD.md's FSI hour figures
should be read the way §7 says: ordinal only. Its own "three to five times longer in Japanese,
Chinese or Arabic" framing is the defensible one; the absolute hour counts are not.

# Implementation

## 8. The arithmetic that decides everything

**INFERENCE, not a finding — assumptions stated.** Steady-state SRS load ≈ (mature cards) ÷ (mean
interval in days). Mature vocabulary cards under FSRS settle around a 60–150 day mean; take 90 days
and ~8 seconds per review.

| Level held, per language | Reviews/day | Minutes/day | × 10 languages |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 words (floor) | ~11 | ~1.5 | **~15 min/day** |
| 2,000 words (conversation possible) | ~22 | ~3 | ~30 min/day |
| 5,000 words (95% coverage, usable) | ~56 | ~7.5 | **~75 min/day** |
| 8,000 words (unassisted reading) | ~100 | ~13 | ~2 hr/day |

`README.md` currently budgets **~15 min/day** to keep all ten warm. The arithmetic says that is
correct — **and only at the 1,000-word floor**, which per §2 buys under 80% coverage, i.e. the
ability to understand nothing unassisted. It is also precisely the level the attrition literature
predicts will not survive: Tracy-Ventura et al. 2025 found advanced learners lost almost nothing
over six years with limited exposure, and that **higher peak attainment predicted less attrition**
(β = −0.44, p = .027). Skills parked high are nearly free. Skills parked low evaporate and the
maintenance was wasted.

And that table is vocabulary only — no input, no speaking, no grammar.

**The conclusion this forces:** ten simultaneously-maintained languages is not a budget problem,
it is a structural one. The honest options are (a) ten languages at a floor that decays and buys
nothing, or (b) a small number pushed to a durable level and genuinely kept, with the rest
explicitly parked as *unmaintained* until a slot opens.

**Recommendation: cap the portfolio at three kept languages.** One in push mode, two in
maintenance, seven parked with no daily obligation and no guilt. Rotate a parked language into push
mode only when the current push reaches its durable level — not on a calendar.

## 9. Which language to push first

**Recommendation: Spanish.** The reasoning is strategic, not sentimental:

- **Category I** — the fastest route to a *parked durable level*, which is the entire strategy above.
- **The input engine is already solved.** Dreaming Spanish is the largest graded comprehensible-input
  library in any language (7,000+ videos). No other folder here has that.
- **The interaction engine costs $0.** `Spanish/README.md` already notes Spanish is everywhere in
  California. The d = 1.09 / d = 0.83 engines are the expensive ones to buy elsewhere.
- **It unlocks the discount chain** your own `README.md` documents: Spanish → Portuguese → French.
  Three of the ten folders get subsidized by finishing one.

The counter-case is Russian: it was the active push in the 2026-07 files, it has sunk cost, and it
pairs with the Orthodoxy and literature shelves. That is a real argument. If Russian is already
past the 2,000-word mark, finish it — restarting costs more than it saves. If it is not, switch;
the protocol below is identical either way.

## 10. The push-mode day (45–60 min)

| When | What | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Morning, 15–20 min | **Grammar spine**, every exercise closed-book *before* checking | g ≈ 1.06; retrieval, not rereading |
| Dead time (commute, gym, cooking) | **Audio input** — graded → podcasts → native | Free hours; d = 0.46 territory |
| Evening, 20 min | **Captioned video** in the target language, L2 subtitles | Large effect on listening *and* vocabulary |
| Evening, last thing, 10 min | **SRS** | §6 — sleep consolidates vocabulary |
| 2× / week, 30 min | **Tutor (italki)** with the standing instruction below | d = 1.09 / d = 0.83 |
| First fortnight, 10 min/day | **HVPT minimal-pair drills**, many speakers | Beginner g = 0.90; fossilization is permanent |

**The standing tutor instruction — copy this into your first message to any tutor:**

> When I make an error, do not rephrase it correctly and move on. Signal that something was wrong
> and make me produce the correct form myself. I would rather be stopped often than sound polished.

That single instruction is the difference between d = 0.53 (recasts) and d = 0.83 (prompts). It is
the cheapest large effect available anywhere in this document.

## 11. The push-mode phases

**Phase 0 — Weeks 1–2. The sound system.**
Script if the language has one (kana, Cyrillic, the Arabic/Hebrew abjads, characters). HVPT
minimal pairs daily. No vocabulary volume yet. This fortnight prevents permanent damage.

**Phase 1 — Months 1–4. Core 1,000 + the spine + graded input.**
20 new cards/day, **L2→L1 direction**, frequency-ordered → 1,000 families in ~7 weeks. The folder's
fundamental book, concentrated, every exercise closed-book. Graded input daily from day one.
**Start speaking at ~week 6.** Milestone: the book is finished and the first 1,000 are mature.

**Phase 2 — Months 4–12. 1,000 → 5,000, and real conversation.**
**Flip cards to L1→L2** as the spine completes (§3). Sentence cards with audio. Input shifts
graded → native with captions. Two tutor sessions weekly, non-negotiable. Milestone: 95% coverage —
the point where the language becomes usable rather than studied.

**Phase 3 — Push to B2, then park.**
Per each folder's README, B2 is the "fluent" milestone. Then downshift to maintenance: SRS only,
long intervals, zero new cards. Tracy-Ventura et al. 2025 says only speech-rate fluency decays at
this level — so schedule one conversation a month per parked language and let everything else ride.

**Do not open a second push until the current one reaches Phase 3.** That is the whole discipline,
and it is the finding — intensity moderates instructional effect, higher peaks resist attrition,
and low-level portfolios evaporate.

## 12. If-then plans

From `../How To Be Consistent.md` — implementation intentions, d = 0.65 (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006),
the best-evidenced tool for closing the planning-doing gap. Anchor to stable cues, keep them
flexible enough to survive a bad week:

- *If* I sit down with coffee in the morning, *then* I open the grammar book before anything else.
- *If* I start driving or walking anywhere, *then* target-language audio goes on.
- *If* I put my phone on the charger for the night, *then* I do the SRS deck first.
- *If* it is Tuesday or Saturday, *then* the tutor session is already booked and paid for.
- *If* I miss a day, *then* I do the SRS only and resume the spine tomorrow — missing one day does
  not damage habit formation; prolonged breaks do.

## 13. The kill list

Stop, or never start:

- **Apps as a spine.** The best Duolingo study (Smith, Jiang & Peters 2024, *LL&T*) is n = 48,
  ~27 hours, **no control group**, with two of three authors employed by Duolingo. It shows apps
  beat nothing. Nothing in that literature competes with g ≈ 1.06 for instruction.
- **Delaying speech to an hour count** (§1).
- **Tuning the SRS algorithm.** Equal vs expanding is statistically equivalent. Pick FSRS, set 20
  new/day, never think about it again.
- **Looking words up while reading and calling it study.** Search contributed nothing to learning
  (§3). Either flag the word into the deck or keep reading.
- **Resource collecting and app-switching** (already flagged in the Japanese file — still true).
- **Interleaving vocabulary across languages in one session.** Verified null for vocabulary.
- **Treating reading-with-audio as an upgrade** (§5).
- **Maintaining ten languages at 1,000 words each** (§8).
