# Capture prompts

Three days, out loud, speech-to-text on. The output is English (or whatever you
think in) — translation comes later. Rules: real sentences you would actually say,
whole sentences not fragments, and no editing for quality while capturing. A dull
sentence you say twice a week beats an interesting one you will never use.

Aim for 300-500 sentences before the first translation batch. Tag each one with a
topic when you move it into `sentences.csv` — the topic column is what lets you
build an audio track for just the situation you are walking into.

## Narrate the day as it happens

- Waking up and getting ready: what you are doing, what you are about to do.
- Every meal: what you are making, what you want, what you are out of.
- Leaving and arriving: where you are going, how long it takes, why you are late.
- Every time you are annoyed, say why. Complaints are high-frequency and you
  produce them with real feeling, which makes them stick.
- Last thing at night: what you did today, what tomorrow looks like.

## Topic blocks

**Work / study.** What you are working on right now. Explaining your project to
someone who is not technical. Asking for an extension. Saying you disagree with an
approach and why. Status updates. What broke and what you tried. Scheduling.
Saying you do not know yet.

**Yourself.** Where you are from, what you study, what you do, how long you have
been doing it, why you started, what you want next. Your family. How you know the
people you know. What you did last weekend, what you are doing next weekend.

**Opinions.** The five things you argue about most. Why you like something, why
you cannot stand something else. Agreeing partially. Disagreeing without being
rude. Saying something is overrated. Changing your mind mid-sentence.

**Stories you already retell.** Everyone has ten. Write each one out as the six or
eight sentences you actually tell it in. These carry enormous weight — a story you
can tell fluently makes you sound far better than your level.

**Transactions.** Ordering, paying, asking for the bill, sending something back,
asking where something is, asking how much, asking for a receipt, returning
something, booking an appointment, describing a symptom to a doctor, asking a
price down, asking for the wifi password.

**Conversation glue.** The parts that make you sound like a speaker rather than a
translator, and the parts that keep a conversation alive when you are lost:
- Sorry, could you say that again more slowly?
- What does that word mean?
- How do you say this in your language?
- I understood about half of that.
- I've only been learning for a few months.
- Give me a second, I'm thinking.
- Right, exactly, that makes sense, no way, obviously, it depends.
- Anyway, as I was saying, the thing is, to be honest, more or less.

**Questions.** You will spend more of a real conversation asking than answering,
and beginners over-prepare answers. Write thirty questions you would genuinely ask
a stranger, a colleague and a friend.

**Your specific life.** The vocabulary nobody else's course contains: your field,
your hobbies, your commute, your health, your city, the things you own and use
daily. This is the whole point of the method — the sentences no textbook would
ever have given you.

## What not to capture

- Single words. Put them inside a sentence you would say.
- Sentences you have never said in English and never will. "The bureau of my aunt
  is beside the table of my uncle" is the failure mode.
- Anything you only want because it looks impressive. You will not remember it and
  you will not use it.
